Solar filament segmentation

Benchmark · results online · 2026

Why: it started as a hackathon entry. The deadline forced a design where every arm changes exactly one thing — and that discipline is what turned a competition entry into a benchmark worth keeping.

Can a vision foundation model pretrained on everyday photographs segment thin, faint filaments on the Sun better than a CNN trained from scratch — and how much of the encoder has to change? Six architectures on 958 GONG full-disk images (MAGFiLO annotations), 5 folds × 3 seeds, one factor changed per arm. A second stage then pretrains the winning encoder on 100,000 unlabelled disks of the same instrument.

Result: pretraining bought efficiency, not accuracy. A SAM 2 encoder with 0.43 % of its weights adapted by LoRA matches the best from-scratch model while training 7× fewer weights, and beats full fine-tuning of the same encoder by 0.03 Dice.

Seismic fault segmentation

Benchmark · results online · 2026

Why: solar filaments and seismic faults pose the same segmentation problem in different data — thin, low-contrast features that occupy a tiny fraction of the image and that interpreters still pick by hand. After the filament benchmark the question was whether the recipe carries to a domain with entirely different physics, or whether it was tuned to the Sun.

How much of a DINOv3 encoder has to change to segment faults — thin, tilted breaks in seismic images? Frozen, LayerNorm-only, LoRA (r = 4 / 16 / 64) and full fine-tuning, all under one recipe, trained on synthetic FaultSeg3D volumes and tested on held-out volumes and on real CRACKS field sections (zero-shot and after a few real labels). A from-scratch U-Net is the control.

Result: on synthetic data accuracy rises with how much of the encoder is trained (IoU 0.61 frozen → 0.66 LoRA → 0.71 full). On real data the order reverses: the lightly adapted models transfer best and the fully fine-tuned one is worse and unstable across seeds. With 200 training volumes a 7.8 M U-Net ties LoRA-DINOv3; with 10 it collapses (0.47 vs 0.55) — the foundation model buys label efficiency, not peak accuracy.

Surface-velocity tracking from UAV river footage

In progress · 2026

Why: graduate research — the computer-vision half of a hydrology problem. Measuring river flow normally means putting an instrument in the water; if the surface can be tracked accurately enough from a drone, the physics-informed inversion downstream has something to run on without anyone getting wet.

Measuring how fast a river flows without putting an instrument in the water. A drone hovers over a reach and films the surface; natural tracers (leaves, foam, debris) are segmented and tracked frame to frame — each tracer gets a temporal mask, a bounding box, an ID and a velocity — and the resulting paths are converted from pixels to metres per second through the georeferenced orthomosaic. The output is a sparse surface-velocity field along the tracer trajectories.

Why it matters: the surface velocities feed a downstream pipeline (power-law / entropy conversion to depth-averaged velocity, then a physics-informed neural network) that inverts channel bathymetry from video alone, cross-checked against ADCP and ADV field measurements and a PIVlab particle-image-velocimetry run on the same footage.

YourEyes — indoor navigation by voice

Team project · Jun 2026 – Present

Why: it began as a class project the team wanted to take further into a product. Indoors, where GPS stops being useful, a blind user has no equivalent of the turn-by-turn guidance sighted people take for granted — so the question was whether a phone's own camera and sensors could close that gap.

An iPhone app that walks a blind user to a room inside a building, entirely by voice. The user says where they want to go; the phone sends bursts of camera frames together with true heading and altitude to a FastAPI backend, which works out where they are standing by matching those frames against stored reference photographs using DINOv2 embeddings, and answers with the next instruction — a compass heading and a step count. Obstacle warnings run on the phone itself, from the LiDAR depth map, so they keep working even when the network does not.

How the building is mapped: the floor is represented as a graph of anchor nodes — points in the corridor where reference photographs were taken, each stored with its GPS position, altitude and the heading the camera was facing. Localisation is a nearest-neighbour search over those embeddings (pgvector, cosine) narrowed first by a spatial filter, and aggregated across the three frames in a burst so that one bad frame cannot move the user.
